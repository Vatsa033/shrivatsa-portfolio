01 / CRICKET

A game that
stayed.

From school grounds to zonal cricket and eventually the RVCE team, cricket ran alongside most of my education — not as a side activity, but as the thing I kept choosing to do.

The position

Left-arm medium pace.
Middle order.

Most of my school life was spent around cricket. It was simply what I enjoyed doing most as a kid — playing, training and coming back to the ground again.

At 13, I started taking the game seriously and joined professional cricket. From there, the sport became a structured part of my life, with training and competition shaping how I approached the game.

Two roles. One mindset: stay patient, read the situation, and contribute wherever the team needs it.
Before engineering

Learning to compete
as a team.

I played U16 to U19 zonal cricket for the Raichur Zone. Those years took cricket beyond simply enjoying the game — they introduced me to the discipline of preparation, tournament pressure and the responsibility that comes with being part of a squad.

The team environment became as important as the game itself: showing up prepared, understanding your role and staying switched on even when the spotlight was somewhere else.

Before the degree

The kit bag came
with me.

By the time I entered engineering, cricket was already a significant part of who I was. The years before RVCE had given me a foundation in competitive sport — routine, repetition, resilience and learning how to perform with other people depending on you.

Engineering changed what I was studying. It did not change what I kept doing outside the classroom.

The transition

A new campus.
Same game.

When I joined RVCE, I got into the college cricket team and carried the sport into engineering. The setting changed — new teammates, new opponents and a new level of competition — but the rhythm was familiar.

We took part in inter-college competitions and VTU tournaments, balancing cricket with the demands of an engineering degree.

Engineering happened in classrooms and labs. A different kind of learning happened on the field.
The moments that stay

Not just the
scoreboard.

There are the obvious moments — the trophy, the medal, the photographs after a tournament. But what stays longer is everything around them: the early training sessions, the conversations between overs, the pressure of a close game and the people you share it with.

Cricket gave my engineering years a second rhythm — one built around competition, consistency and belonging to a team.

What cricket left behind

Play your role.
Trust the team.

Cricket was one of the constants through school and engineering. It taught me to prepare, adapt to the situation, stay composed under pressure and understand that individual performance only matters in the context of the team.

02 / WESTERN DANCE

Western dance
team.

Part of Enigma, the Western Dance Team at RVCE. What began as another thing to do in college became a journey through stages, competitions, travel and a team that became a big part of engineering life.

Different discipline. Same idea: prepare hard, know your role, and make the whole team better.

From college stages
to bigger rooms.

Competitions took us well beyond the familiar college circuit. IIT Madras was one of those stages where the scale, preparation and standard of performance felt noticeably different.

We finished 3rd at IIT Madras in 2024 — another marker in a journey that kept getting more competitive.

03 / BUSINESS MARATHON

From an idea
to first place.

During the Entrepreneurship Summit at RVCE, our team took part in the Business Marathon — developing Vintage Wheels, a one-stop platform for vintage and retro car owners.

The result
01FIRST PLACE

What started as a problem worth solving became a complete business concept — and ultimately the winning project of the Business Marathon.

Vintage Wheels — “Preserving the past. Keeping it on the road.”
011st place · Business Marathon
04Team members
01Platform concept
03Core value proposition pillars
01 / The problem

Keeping an old car
on the road.

The idea started with a simple observation: vintage and retro cars can be difficult to maintain and restore as original parts become rare, expensive and harder to source.

Owners also face uncertainty around trustworthy manufacturers, specialist maintenance knowledge and counterfeit components — while traditional servicing channels may no longer support older vehicles.

02 / The solution

One place to
find the answer.

We proposed Vintage Wheels as a website and mobile app connecting owners with authentic spare parts, maintenance support and expert guidance.

The platform would act as a bridge between car owners, manufacturers, workshops, experts, clubs and potential buyers — bringing a fragmented ecosystem into one place.

Find. Verify. Connect.
01Verified parts

Work with reliable manufacturers and test outsourced products against defined quality requirements.

02Expert guidance

Give owners access to specialists for maintenance, restoration and repair guidance.

03Community

Connect retro car clubs, workshops and enthusiasts to share practical knowledge and resources.

04Simple platform

Keep the website and app intuitive while connecting owners to the right solution.

03 / How it works

A simple journey
for the owner.

The proposed experience was deliberately straightforward: identify the car and requirement, find the relevant parts or service, verify quality, then connect and transact.

The business model could grow beyond the basic platform through an online marketplace, manufacturer and workshop partnerships, retro-car communities and additional restoration and maintenance services.

04 / The value proposition

Find.
Trust.
Connect.

The concept was built around reducing the uncertainty vintage-car owners face: finding genuine parts and specialist services, trusting the quality and expertise behind them, and connecting with the people who can keep the vehicle moving.

What the marathon left behind

Think beyond
the idea.

The Business Marathon was less about having a perfect business on day one and more about taking a problem, structuring it, building a solution and communicating why it should exist.

04 / SCHOOL TEACHING

Give knowledge
a place to grow.

From July 2024 to January 2025, I volunteered with a team from RVCE to conduct teaching sessions for sixth-grade students at the Government Medium Primary School, Kengeri.

The initiative

Eight sessions.
One classroom.

Across eight sessions, we worked with both Kannada and English divisions of the sixth grade, covering English, Science and Mathematics through explanations, exercises, games and hands-on activities.

The goal was simple: make difficult ideas easier to understand — and make learning something the students wanted to participate in.
08Teaching sessions
80Hours of engagement
60Sixth-grade students
02Science models donated
01 / Teaching

Teach the concept.
Then make it stick.

The sessions moved between multiplication tables, number comparisons, geometry, angles, fractions, integers, English reading and grammar. When language became a barrier, concepts were re-explained in Kannada or Hindi so students could follow the underlying idea rather than simply memorize an answer.

We used textbook exercises, board work, reading aloud, real-life examples and individual guidance to meet students at their level.

02 / Beyond the lesson

Learning worked
both ways.

The sessions were deliberately interactive. Pictionary, quizzes, quick-response games and group activities helped turn lessons into something more engaging while reinforcing the concepts being taught.

The experience also developed our own leadership, patience, empathy and communication — especially the ability to simplify an idea without losing what matters.

Community engagement became another form of engineering thinking: understand the user, adapt the explanation, test understanding, and iterate.
03 / Leaving something behind

From a lesson
to a resource.

We built and donated physical models of the eye and respiratory system so the school could continue using visual and tactile teaching aids after the sessions ended.

The respiratory-system sessions used working models, balloons and simple demonstrations to make breathing and airflow tangible. The donated models were intended to support future science lessons as well.

What the experience left behind

Explain clearly.
Listen better.

The report records improvements in students' reading, writing, comprehension, mathematical problem-solving and curiosity in science. For me, the bigger takeaway was learning how much patience, empathy and adaptability matter when you are responsible for making someone else understand.
